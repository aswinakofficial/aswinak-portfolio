# Some trips begin with a carefully planned itinerary.

Ours began with one trek, ended up becoming another, involved a 240 km scooter ride, and somehow still worked out.

We had originally planned to trek **Netravati Peak in Karnataka**. The plan was to travel from Kerala to Mangalore by train, stay near the base camp and use public transport for the rest of the journey.

Then, just before the trip, Netravati was closed because of administrative issues.

So we changed the plan.

And that's how we ended up doing the **Kudremukh trek from Kerala**.

Looking back, the unexpected change probably made the trip more memorable.

***

## The Booking Problem

Our initial idea was to plan the trip ourselves and keep the cost low.

For Karnataka forest treks, the usual approach is to book a trek slot through the official online system. We knew the slots opened in advance, so I was ready to book as soon as our date became available.

It didn't go well.

The booking window opened, and the website became almost impossible to access. I kept trying for the first couple of minutes, and when I finally got through, there were no slots left.

They had disappeared within seconds.

I even experimented with automating the repetitive form-filling process to make the booking faster. But that didn't solve the real problem. The website itself was struggling under the traffic.

At the time, there were also complaints from individual trekkers about bulk bookings by package operators. I can't independently verify exactly how much that influenced the availability, but it was a common concern among people trying to book.

Eventually, the system was shut down/reworked for a period.

Since we couldn't get the trek slot ourselves, we approached a package provider near the base camp and paid around **₹2,400–₹2,500 per person**, including the trek arrangement, accommodation and food.

Later, after speaking with the homestay owner directly, we learned that arranging the stay and other services ourselves could potentially have cost considerably less.

That was frustrating.

We hadn't chosen a package because we wanted convenience. We chose it because we couldn't get the ticket.

So, if you're planning a similar trek, my advice from this experience is simple: **try booking the trek yourself first, but keep a backup plan.**

We definitely needed one.

***

## Kerala to Mangalore

We travelled overnight on the **Maveli Express (16604)** and reached Mangalore around 9 AM after a slight delay.

After breakfast, we went to collect our rental scooter from Kankanady.

Our original plan was to use buses from Mangalore, but once we looked at the return timings, it didn't seem practical. We would finish the trek late in the afternoon and still need to get back to Mangalore.

So we rented a scooter for around **₹500**.

The plan was simple:

**120 km from Mangalore to Samse, one night there, the trek the next day, and 120 km back.**

Simple on paper.

A little less simple in reality.

***

## The Ride to Samse

The ride from Mangalore to Samse was long, but I genuinely enjoyed it.

There was road construction in several places, so some sections were dusty and rough. Then it started raining, and the whole route changed character.

The greenery, forest roads and rain made the ride feel like a proper road trip.

We stopped at a roadside restaurant for lunch and continued towards Samse.

One small thing I noticed during the journey was how different some everyday things felt compared with Kerala. We came across several liquor shops that looked almost like modern supermarkets, and at one restaurant we noticed alcohol being served even though the place didn't immediately appear to be a conventional bar.

It was a small observation, but that's one of the things I like about travelling: even a familiar destination can have little differences that catch you off guard.

***

## An Evening in Samse

We reached Samse at around **3 PM**, a day before the trek.

We had a free evening, so we walked around and looked for places to visit.

There isn't a huge list of must-see attractions around the village, at least from what we experienced. We came across a temple surrounded by tea plantations, and we also heard about an old Jain temple nearby that we didn't get to visit.

We had planned to use the scooter to explore a few attractions on the way, but several were closed during our visit. Hanuman Gundi Waterfall and some other waterfall spots weren't accessible, and even a dam we came across was closed.

So, in the end, the road itself became the highlight.

And honestly, I was okay with that.

***

## The Night Before the Trek

Our stay was comfortable enough, although I wasn't particularly impressed with the food.

Dinner was rice, watery sambar and dal curry. Breakfast the next morning was idli and sambar, which was better, but still nothing memorable.

The thing I remember more clearly from that evening was our failed attempt to find a midnight chai.

In Kerala, going out late at night for tea is pretty normal for us, especially when we're travelling. So we asked the locals where we could get one.

Their response was basically:

**“This isn't Kerala.”**

It was too late, there wouldn't be anywhere open, and we were better off going back to sleep.

Fair enough.

We did exactly that.

***

# Kudremukh Trek: The Actual Adventure

We were ready by **6 AM**.

A pickup vehicle came to take us towards the trekking base, and even that short ride felt like part of the adventure. More than ten people were standing in the back, and we had to keep ducking under branches and leaves.

We were half-joking about watching out for green snakes too.

The trek hadn't even started yet, and we were already having fun.

At the forest entrance, there was a checkpost where we had to take the required vehicle pass. Based on our experience, it cost around ₹40 per vehicle. There was another checkpoint on the Samse side, where the pass had to be handed over. The process was reversed while leaving.

The exact rules and fees can change, so this is based on what we experienced during our trip.

***

## 22 Kilometres Through the Hills

The trek was roughly **22 km round trip**, about 11 km each way.

For me, the difficulty felt moderate.

The distance was the bigger challenge.

And then we got rain.

The wet soil made several sections extremely slippery. People were falling everywhere. Some of the falls were funny at the time, but they could easily have gone wrong, especially around rocks or steeper sections.

Proper trekking shoes make a big difference here.

One guy in our group was incredibly tall—almost seven feet, by our estimate—and he struggled on a few sections and fell more than ten times.

We laughed a lot.

But the trail deserved respect.

Even with proper shoes, it was easy to lose your balance.

The scenery, though, was beautiful.

The landscape kept opening up as we climbed, with lush green hills, forests and misty stretches making the effort feel worthwhile.

***

## Lunch at the Peak

We reached the peak somewhere between **11 AM and noon**.

And the view was incredible.

There was a flag at the top, but honestly, it was the landscape that caught our attention.

We had planned to eat our lunch there, but we'd made one small mistake in our planning.

We assumed that because lunch was included in our package, lunch boxes would be provided too.

They weren't.

We had to arrange our own lunch boxes.

We could easily have brought empty reusable ones from Kerala, so this became an unnecessary expense.

That's something I'd definitely do differently next time: **carry your own reusable lunch box.**

We packed the food in the morning, carried it to the peak and finally sat down to eat.

After several hours of walking, lunch tastes different when you're sitting on top of a mountain looking across miles of green.

We spent some time taking photos and enjoying the view before starting the descent.

***

## A Small Lesson About Water

There were occasional water sources along parts of the trail, including small streams and waterfalls.

I used some natural water to wash my face and even drank a little, but I wouldn't recommend drinking untreated water unless you know it's safe. It may contain contaminants that you're simply not used to.

For me, the more important lesson was **using drinking water carefully**.

We carried water from the base, and after drinking through the climb, I didn't have much left at the peak.

After lunch, I wanted to wash my hands.

I ended up using just a little water and then drying them with a towel.

It worked, but it wasn't exactly comfortable.

So carry enough water for the whole day, not just for the climb to the top.

And remember that whatever snacks you carry—protein bars, energy bars or anything else—the wrapper comes back with you.

The forest doesn't need our plastic.

***

## The Race Back to Mangalore

We started descending after spending enough time at the peak and reached the base around **5 PM**.

That's when we realized the trek wasn't actually the last challenge of the day.

We still had **120 km to ride back to Mangalore**.

And the rental shop closed at **10 PM**.

If we missed that deadline, we'd have to keep the scooter overnight and return it the next morning.

So there wasn't much time to relax.

We got ready and started riding.

The morning journey had been enjoyable and scenic.

The evening journey was different.

We were watching the clock.

After a long trek, 120 km on a scooter isn't exactly what you dream about. But we had no choice.

And somehow, we made it.

We returned the scooter at almost exactly **10 PM**.

That felt like completing a second challenge after the trek itself.

***

## Back to Kerala

After returning the scooter, we took an Uber to the railway station.

We boarded **Train 12618** around 11:40 PM and finally had a chance to relax.

We reached Kochi around **8 AM the next morning**.

And just like that, the trip was over.

***

# What I'd Recommend for a Kudremukh Trek

The most useful thing I can say after this trip is that the trek itself isn't the only thing you need to plan.

Think about the entire journey.

If you're travelling from Kerala, reaching Mangalore is only the beginning. You still need to figure out how you're getting to Samse, where you're staying, how you're getting to the trailhead and—most importantly—how you're getting back after walking 22 km.

A bus can work if your trek finishes early enough and the current schedules line up.

For us, the rental scooter made more sense because we wanted flexibility.

But that flexibility comes with responsibility. We had to cover 120 km after the trek and return the vehicle before 10 PM.

That isn't something I'd recommend doing casually.

Also, carry your own lunch box, proper trekking shoes, enough water, a few energy snacks and a small bag for your waste.

And don't depend on natural water sources unless you're certain they're safe to drink.

***

# Was Kudremukh Worth It?

Absolutely.

The funny thing is that Kudremukh wasn't even supposed to happen.

We were supposed to trek Netravati.

Then the trek closed.

We couldn't get the booking we wanted.

We ended up taking a package.

We rented a scooter.

We rode 120 km to Samse.

We stayed in a village where we couldn't find our midnight chai.

We woke up at 6 AM.

We walked 22 km through wet, slippery trails.

We ate lunch at the peak.

Then we walked back down and rode another 120 km to Mangalore.

It sounds exhausting when I write it down.

And it was.

But it was also one of those trips where all the little inconveniences become part of the story.

The places we couldn't visit.

The average dinner.

The last-minute change of trek.

The slippery trail.

The race against the clock.

All of it is part of what I remember.

And maybe that's the best thing about travelling.

**The trips you remember most aren't always the ones that went according to plan. They're the ones that went wrong in interesting ways.**

Kudremukh was our backup plan.

I'm glad we took it.
